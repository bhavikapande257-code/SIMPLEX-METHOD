Workflow · Methodology

How it Works

Two flavours of the simplex method — the classic Big-M penalty approach, and the numerically cleaner Two-Phase method — explained step by step.

The Process

Four Steps to a Solution

From entering your problem to reading an exact fractional answer — here is what happens under the hood at each stage.

01
Define your LP
Enter the number of decision variables and constraints. Fill in coefficients for the objective function and each constraint row, choose the inequality sign (≤, =, ≥), and set variable bounds — non-negative, non-positive, or free (unrestricted).
02
Add auxiliary variables
Slack, surplus, and artificial variables are inserted automatically to bring the LP into standard equality form. In Big-M mode a large penalty is applied to artificials; in Two-Phase mode a separate Phase 1 objective minimises their sum to find a basic feasible starting point.
03
Iterate the tableau
At every iteration the full ratio column is displayed alongside the tableau. The most-negative reduced cost selects the entering variable; the minimum ratio test selects the leaving variable. The pivot element is highlighted in amber, driving exact row operations to update the basis.
04
Read the solution
When all reduced costs are non-negative (for maximisation) the algorithm halts. Variable values and the objective are reported as exact fractions — guaranteed free of floating-point rounding error. Infeasibility and unboundedness are detected and reported with diagnostic messages.
Supported Methods

Big-M vs Two-Phase

Both methods handle the same class of problems. Choose the one that suits your pedagogical or computational preference.

Method I
Big-M Method
Artificial variables are penalised by a large constant M in the objective function. A single-phase simplex run drives them out of the basis if a feasible solution exists.
  • Single unified tableau from start to finish
  • Straightforward to set up by hand
  • Classic textbook formulation (Dantzig, 1947)
  • Best for small problems and classroom demonstration
Method II
Two-Phase Method
Phase 1 minimises the sum of artificial variables to zero, establishing a basic feasible solution. Phase 2 then optimises the original objective from that starting point.
  • Numerically cleaner — avoids large M coefficients
  • Detects infeasibility at the end of Phase 1
  • Preferred for larger or ill-conditioned problems
  • Clearly separates feasibility from optimality
Standard Form Reference

The LP in Standard Form

Every problem entered into the solver is internally converted to this canonical structure before the simplex algorithm begins.

Objective
Maximise Z = c₁x₁ + c₂x₂ + … + cₙxₙ
Subject to
a₁₁x₁ + a₁₂x₂ ≤ b₁
a₂₁x₁ + a₂₂x₂ ≥ b₂
a₃₁x₁ + a₃₂x₂ = b₃
Variable Bounds
xᵢ ≥ 0 (default)
xᵢ ≤ 0 (non-positive)
xᵢ unrestricted
